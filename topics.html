01 Foundation

Matter & Its States

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. The three classical states — solid, liquid, and gas — differ in how strongly their particles interact and how much freedom of motion those particles have.

A fourth state, plasma, exists at extremely high temperatures and consists of ionised particles. It is the most abundant form of visible matter in the universe.

Solid
close-packed
Fixed shape and volume. Particles vibrate in fixed positions.
Liquid
H₂O (l)
Fixed volume, takes container shape. Particles flow past each other.
Gas
H₂O (g)
No fixed shape or volume. Particles move freely and rapidly.
Did you know?

Water is one of the few substances less dense as a solid than as a liquid. This is why ice floats — and why aquatic life can survive beneath frozen lake surfaces in winter.

Key Concept
Phase changes occur at constant temperature — all energy goes into changing arrangement, not raising temperature.
  • MeltingSolid → Liquid (endothermic)
  • FreezingLiquid → Solid (exothermic)
  • VaporisationLiquid → Gas (endothermic)
  • CondensationGas → Liquid (exothermic)
  • SublimationSolid → Gas directly (e.g. dry ice)
⚡ Why does water expand when it freezes?

Hydrogen bonds in liquid water are constantly breaking and forming — molecules are close-packed. When ice forms, H-bonds LOCK into a hexagonal lattice with gaps — less dense. This is why ice floats and aquatic life survives under frozen surfaces in winter. No other common substance does this.

⚠ Common Mistake

Students think boiling point depends only on molecular mass. Wrong — intermolecular forces matter more. Water (M=18, bp=100°C) boils far higher than H₂S (M=34, bp=−60°C) because of stronger hydrogen bonding, not mass.

02 Atomic Structure

Inside the Atom

The atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical identity. It consists of a nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in shells or orbitals.

The atomic number (Z) equals the number of protons and defines the element. The mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron counts.

Z = number of protons
A = Z + N (neutrons)
Isotopes: same Z, different A

Electrons occupy energy levels (shells). The Aufbau principle states that electrons fill the lowest available energy level first. Hund's rule and the Pauli exclusion principle further govern how electrons populate orbitals.

  • Proton+1 charge, in nucleus. Determines element identity.
  • NeutronNo charge, in nucleus. Determines isotope.
  • Electron−1 charge, in shells/orbitals around nucleus.
  • OrbitalRegion of space where electron is likely found (s, p, d, f).
⚡ Why do electrons occupy specific energy levels?

Electrons are waves (de Broglie: λ = h/mv). They can only exist where their wave fits perfectly around the nucleus — like a standing wave on a guitar string. Only certain wavelengths fit: 1 half-wavelength, 2 half-wavelengths, etc. — these are the energy levels. This is WHY they're quantised, not arbitrary.

⚠ Common Mistake

Atomic number = protons (defines the element). Mass number = protons + neutrons. Isotopes have SAME atomic number, DIFFERENT mass number. ¹²C and ¹⁴C are both carbon (6 protons) — different neutron counts. Mass number is NOT atomic mass (which is a weighted average of isotopes).

03 Chemical Bonding

What holds matter together

Ionic Bond

Electron Transfer

Formed when one atom transfers electrons to another, creating oppositely charged ions that attract. Example: NaCl (Na⁺ and Cl⁻).

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Covalent Bond

Electron Sharing

Formed when atoms share electron pairs. Can be single (σ), double, or triple bonds. Example: H₂O, CO₂, N₂.

H–H: shared pair
O=C=O: double bonds
Metallic Bond

Sea of Electrons

Metal atoms release their valence electrons to form a shared "electron sea." This gives metals their conductivity, malleability, and lustre.

Cu: 1 free e⁻/atom
Al: 3 free e⁻/atom
VSEPR Theory

The shape of a molecule is determined by the repulsion between electron pairs around the central atom. Water (H₂O) is bent, not linear, because its two lone pairs push the hydrogen atoms closer together — giving it a 104.5° bond angle.

WHY do ionic bonds form?
Na gives 1 electron to Cl not out of "generosity" — but because the lattice energy released (−787 kJ/mol) far exceeds the ionisation energy cost (+496 kJ/mol). Energy drives the process. Always ask: is the total energy change favourable?
⚠ Common Mistake — Polarity vs Polar

CO₂ has TWO polar C=O bonds. But it's a NON-POLAR molecule because it's linear — the two dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel. H₂O has polar O-H bonds AND is polar because it's bent (104.5°) — dipoles don't cancel. Bond polarity ≠ molecule polarity.

04 Acids, Bases & Salts

The Chemistry of pH

Acids are proton donors (Brønsted–Lowry) or electron-pair acceptors (Lewis). Bases are proton acceptors or electron-pair donors. Their reaction produces a salt and water — neutralisation.

The pH scale runs from 0 (strongly acidic) to 14 (strongly basic), with 7 being neutral. Each unit change represents a 10-fold change in H⁺ concentration.

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]
pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]
pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C)

Buffer solutions resist changes in pH. They typically consist of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid). Biological systems depend critically on pH buffering — blood pH must remain between 7.35 and 7.45.

pH Scale
Stomach acid: pH ≈ 1.5. Lemon juice: pH ≈ 2. Blood: pH ≈ 7.4. Bleach: pH ≈ 13.
  • Strong acidFully dissociates in water (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄)
  • Weak acidPartially dissociates (CH₃COOH, HF)
  • NeutralisationAcid + Base → Salt + Water
  • TitrationMeasuring concentration using a known-strength reagent
  • IndicatorsLitmus, phenolphthalein — change colour with pH
⚡ Why is HF a weak acid despite F being the most electronegative?

Electronegativity makes F pull electrons strongly — but it also makes the H-F bond very short and STRONG (bond energy 568 kJ/mol vs H-Cl 432 kJ/mol). This strong bond resists dissociation in water. So HF is a WEAK acid (Ka = 6.8×10⁻⁴) despite fluorine's electronegativity. Acid strength depends on H-X bond energy, not just electronegativity.

⚠ Strong ≠ Concentrated

"Strong" acid = fully dissociates (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄). "Weak" acid = partially dissociates (CH₃COOH, HF, H₂CO₃). "Concentrated" = high moles per litre. A concentrated weak acid can have lower pH than a dilute strong acid. These are completely independent concepts.

05 Thermochemistry

Energy in Reactions

Every chemical reaction involves energy changes. Exothermic reactions release energy (ΔH < 0); endothermic reactions absorb it (ΔH > 0). These changes are measured in kJ/mol.

Hess's Law states that the total enthalpy change is path-independent — it depends only on initial and final states. This allows calculation of ΔH for reactions that can't be measured directly.

ΔH = Σ ΔHf°(products) − Σ ΔHf°(reactants)
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
If ΔG < 0: reaction is spontaneous
Did you know?

When you burn 1 mole of methane (CH₄), it releases 890 kJ — enough to boil approximately 4.5 litres of water from room temperature.

  • Enthalpy (H)Heat content at constant pressure
  • Entropy (S)Measure of disorder / randomness
  • Gibbs (G)G = H − TS; predicts spontaneity
  • ExothermicEnergy released; ΔH negative (e.g. combustion)
  • EndothermicEnergy absorbed; ΔH positive (e.g. photosynthesis)
  • Bond energyEnergy to break/form one mole of bonds
  • Hess's LawΔH is path-independent
Spontaneity
A reaction is spontaneous when ΔG < 0. This depends on both enthalpy and entropy — a reaction can be spontaneous even if endothermic, if entropy increases enough.
⚡ Why can an endothermic reaction be spontaneous?

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. If ΔS is positive (entropy increases) and T is high enough, TΔS can exceed ΔH, making ΔG negative (spontaneous) even if ΔH > 0. Dissolving NH₄NO₃ in water is endothermic (feels cold) but spontaneous at room temperature — entropy increases as ordered crystal becomes dispersed ions.

⚠ Hess's Law Sign Error

When reversing a reaction for Hess's Law, REVERSE THE SIGN of ΔH. If A→B has ΔH = −200 kJ/mol, then B→A has ΔH = +200 kJ/mol. This trips up many students in exam calculations. Always check: ΔH_forward = −ΔH_reverse.

06 Organic Chemistry

The Chemistry of Carbon

Carbon's ability to form four bonds and chain with itself creates the enormous diversity of organic molecules — from simple methane to DNA.

Alkanes

CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Single bonds only. Saturated. Methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈). Used as fuels.

Alkenes

CₙH₂ₙ

At least one C=C double bond. Unsaturated. Ethene (C₂H₄), propene. Undergo addition reactions.

Alcohols

R–OH

Contain hydroxyl group (–OH). Methanol, ethanol. Undergo oxidation, esterification. Water-soluble.

Carboxylic Acids

R–COOH

Acidic functional group. Ethanoic acid (vinegar), amino acids. Form esters with alcohols.

Aromatic

C₆H₆ (Benzene)

Delocalised π electrons. Special stability (aromaticity). Undergo substitution reactions, not addition.

Isomers

Same formula, different structure

Structural isomers have different connectivity. Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangement.

07 Stoichiometry

The Mole Concept

The mole is the SI base unit for amount of substance. One mole of any substance contains exactly 6.022 × 10²³ particles — Avogadro's number. This bridges the atomic world (where we count individual atoms) and the laboratory world (where we weigh grams).

The molar mass of a substance (in g/mol) is numerically equal to its relative atomic or molecular mass. For water (H₂O): M = 2(1.008) + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol. So 18.015 g of water contains exactly one mole — 6.022 × 10²³ molecules.

n = m / M    (moles = mass ÷ molar mass)
N = n × Nₐ    (number of particles)
Nₐ = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

Stoichiometry uses mole ratios from balanced equations to calculate how much of each substance is consumed or produced. The coefficients in a balanced equation directly give the mole ratios.

Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O tells us 2 mol H₂ reacts with 1 mol O₂ to produce 2 mol H₂O. If you start with 4 g of H₂ (= 2 mol), you need exactly 1 mol (32 g) of O₂.

Scale of Avogadro's Number

If you had one mole of seconds, it would be 19 quadrillion years — over a million times the age of the universe. Yet chemists work routinely with moles of atoms in a single test tube.

Why the Mole?
The mole makes chemistry calculable at human scale — it links atomic mass units (invisible) to grams (weighable).
  • Mole (mol)Amount of substance; 6.022 × 10²³ particles
  • Molar massMass of 1 mol in g/mol; numerically = relative mass
  • Avogadro (Nₐ)6.022 × 10²³ — particles per mole
  • Empirical formulaSimplest whole-number atom ratio
  • Molecular formulaActual number of atoms per molecule
  • Limiting reagentReactant that runs out first; determines yield
  • % yield(actual/theoretical) × 100
