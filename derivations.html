Formula Derivations

Understanding where equations come from is more powerful than memorising them. Each derivation is broken into clear, logical steps.

DERIVATION 01
Ideal Gas Equation
PV = nRT
Gases · Thermodynamics
  • 01
    Boyle's Law: at constant T and n, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.P ∝ 1/V → PV = k₁
  • 02
    Charles's Law: at constant P and n, volume is directly proportional to temperature.V ∝ T → V/T = k₂
  • 03
    Avogadro's Law: at constant T and P, volume is proportional to moles.V ∝ n → V/n = k₃
  • 04
    Combining all three proportionalities:V ∝ nT/P → V = R·nT/P
  • 05
    Rearranging gives the Ideal Gas Law. R = 8.314 J/mol·K is the universal gas constant.PV = nRT ✓
Where R comes from: R = Nₐ × kB = 6.022×10²³ × 1.381×10⁻²³ = 8.314 J/mol·K. It links the macroscopic gas constant to the Boltzmann constant at the molecular level.
When it fails: The ideal gas law assumes molecules have no volume and no intermolecular forces. Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature — corrected by the van der Waals equation: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.
DERIVATION 02
pH Scale
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]
Acid-Base Chemistry
  • 01
    Water undergoes self-ionisation (autoionisation) to a small extent:H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻
  • 02
    At 25°C, the ionic product of water Kw is measured experimentally:Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol²/L²
  • 03
    In pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ mol/L. The [H⁺] range in chemistry is 10⁰ to 10⁻¹⁴ — an enormous range to handle linearly.
  • 04
    Sørensen (1909) proposed using −log₁₀ to compress this into a convenient 0–14 scale:pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]
  • 05
    Since pKw = pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C):pOH = 14 − pH
Physical meaning: Each unit change in pH represents a 10-fold change in [H⁺]. pH 5 has 10× more H⁺ than pH 6, and 100× more than pH 7.
Temperature dependence: pH 7 is neutral only at 25°C. At 37°C (body temperature), Kw = 2.4×10⁻¹⁴, so neutral pH ≈ 6.8. Blood pH 7.4 is still basic at body temperature.
DERIVATION 03
Gibbs Free Energy
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
Thermodynamics · Spontaneity
  • 01
    Second Law of Thermodynamics: a process is spontaneous if total entropy (system + surroundings) increases:ΔS_total = ΔS_sys + ΔS_surr > 0
  • 02
    At constant T and P, the entropy change of surroundings relates to enthalpy:ΔS_surr = −ΔH_sys / T
  • 03
    Substituting into the total entropy condition:ΔS_sys − ΔH/T > 0
  • 04
    Multiply through by −T (flip inequality since T > 0):ΔH − TΔS_sys < 0
  • 05
    Define Gibbs Free Energy G = H − TS. Then for a spontaneous process:ΔG = ΔH − TΔS < 0 ✓
Physical meaning: ΔG represents the maximum useful work a system can do. When ΔG = 0, the system is at equilibrium — no net work is available.
Link to equilibrium: ΔG° = −RT ln K. A large equilibrium constant K means ΔG° is very negative — the reaction strongly favours products.
DERIVATION 04
Rate Law & Integrated Rate Law
ln[A] = −kt + ln[A]₀
Chemical Kinetics
  • 01
    For a first-order reaction A → products, the rate is proportional to [A]:rate = −d[A]/dt = k[A]
  • 02
    Separate variables to integrate:d[A]/[A] = −k dt
  • 03
    Integrate both sides (left: from [A]₀ to [A]; right: from 0 to t):∫d[A]/[A] = −k∫dt → ln[A] − ln[A]₀ = −kt
  • 04
    Rearrange to the integrated form:ln[A] = −kt + ln[A]₀
  • 05
    Half-life (when [A] = [A]₀/2): ln(½) = −kt½ → t½ = ln2/k = 0.693/k
Graphical test: For first-order reactions, a plot of ln[A] vs time is linear with slope = −k. This is how you determine reaction order from experimental data.
Second-order: rate = k[A]² gives 1/[A] = kt + 1/[A]₀, and t½ = 1/(k[A]₀) — half-life depends on initial concentration.
